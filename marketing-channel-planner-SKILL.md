# Marketing Channel Planner

This skill turns a product knowledge base (or a short product brief) into a **channel mix table** for that product: every relevant **digital** and/or **physical** marketing communication channel (only the type or types the user asks for), with priorities, how each channel serves the user's objective, how to run it, what to measure, and where to start.

It is built on a library of **115 channels (65 digital, 50 physical)** rated for **13 business types**, and on principles from six books: *How Brands Grow* (Byron Sharp), *Sales and Distribution Management* (Panda and Sahadev), *Traction* (Weinberg and Mares), *Product-Led Growth* (Wes Bush), *A Practitioner's Guide to Account-Based Marketing* (Burgess and Munn) and *Content Inc.* (Joe Pulizzi), plus B2B research from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, the LinkedIn B2B Institute and McKinsey.

Reply in the user's language (for example Bangla or English). Channel names can stay in English.

---

## Step 1: Collect the inputs before planning

**Hard rule: no answers, no plan.** Do not produce the channel plan, a draft, a "provisional plan" or any part of it until Step 1 is complete: the product minimum in 1A is in hand **and** the user has answered every basic question in 1B. Never fill a missing answer with your own assumption. Follow this order.

### 1A. Product knowledge base

First read the whole conversation and every attached file or link. Then decide:

| Situation | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| The user has already shared a knowledge base (a file, link, pasted text or an earlier message describing the product in detail) | **Do not ask for it again.** Read it and pull out every input listed in 1C. |
| No knowledge base has been shared | Ask for it: "Please share the product knowledge base (a document, link or text about the product, its customers, pricing and how it is sold)." |
| The user names only a category, not a product (for example "WordPress plugin", "cement", "SaaS") | Ask which product it is, and ask for its knowledge base. If the conversation or memory suggests a likely product, ask the user to confirm it ("Is this for X?"). Never plan for a product you guessed. |
| The user says they have no knowledge base | Ask for a **product brief** instead, using the template in 1D. |
| The user refuses both | Ask for at least: product name, what it is, who buys it, and how it is sold today. Do not plan with less than this. |

### 1B. The basic questions

Check which of these are still unanswered. Ask **only the unanswered ones**, all together in **one message**, numbered, each with short options. You can ask them in the same message as the request for the knowledge base. Never ask a question the knowledge base, the brief or the conversation already answers. "Already answered" means the user or their files actually state it, not that you inferred it.

1. **Market:** Will you market in **Bangladesh** or **abroad**? If abroad, which countries or regions? If both, which comes first?
2. **Channel mix:** Do you want a **digital** channel mix, a **physical** channel mix, or **both**? Only the chosen type will appear in the plan.
3. **Objective:** What do you want to achieve with this channel mix? Pick the main goal (awareness, leads, sales, app installs or sign-ups, retention and repeat buying, wider distribution, or a new launch) and say what success looks like, with a target if you have one (for example "5,000 new installs in 90 days" or "200 new dealers this year"). The objective is required; a number is welcome but not required.
4. **Buyer:** Is it B2C, B2B or both? Who is the main buyer, who uses it, and who influences the choice (for example masons, doctors, mechanics, IT heads, parents)?
5. **Budget and team:** Is the monthly marketing budget low, medium or high (an amount if they are happy to share)? Is the work done in-house, by an agency, or both?
6. **Stage and timeline:** Is this a new launch or an established product? What period should the plan cover (for example 90 days or 12 months)?

Ask these only if they matter and are missing, and mark them "(optional)": channels used today and what worked; main competitors; where the product is sold today. Optional questions do not block the plan; if the user skips them, go ahead once questions 1 to 6 are answered.

Rules:
- **Wait for the answers.** Questions 1 to 6 are required. Until each one has an answer from the user, do not plan, and do not start web research or draft tables for the plan. Before the answers arrive, only read what the user shared (files, pasted text, links they gave).
- **Never assume an answer.** Do not fill a missing market, mix, objective, buyer, budget, stage or timeline from the product type, the user's profile or memory, the website or web research. You may use those facts to suggest a likely option inside the question (for example "WordPress plugins usually sell globally. Is that right for you?"), but the user must confirm it.
- **Partial answers:** if the reply answers only some questions (for example the user replies with just a product name or category), thank them for what they answered and ask again for **only the missing ones**, in one short numbered message. Repeat until every required question is answered. There is no limit on rounds.
- **"Just give me the plan":** if the user asks for the plan while required questions are still open, do not proceed. Say in one line that the plan depends on these answers, and re-ask the open ones in their shortest form, with options to pick from.
- **Explicit delegation is an answer:** if the user explicitly hands a question to you ("you decide", "assume it", "use the default"), that counts as an answer for that question only. Choose a sensible option and list it at the top of the plan as "Decided by agent at your request".
- **Market** and **channel mix** decide the whole plan, so never guess them.
- Keep the questions short and friendly; do not lecture.

### 1C. Inputs to extract

From the knowledge base, brief and answers, note:
product or brand name; what it is; category; price range and how often people buy; B2C or B2B; buyer, user and influencer; geography (urban, rural, national, export); how it is sold today (dealers, shops, supermarkets, online store, marketplace, app store, direct sales, agents); competitors; unique selling points; current channels and results; objective and target; budget; team; timeline; market; channel mix.

### 1D. Product brief template (when there is no knowledge base)

Send this list and ask the user to fill in what they can:

1. Brand or product name, and what it is in one line
2. Category (what kind of product or service)
3. Who buys it and who uses it (B2C or B2B; age, income, location, job role or company type)
4. Who influences the purchase
5. Price range and how often people buy
6. How it is sold today (dealers, retail shops, online store, marketplace, app store, direct sales, agents)
7. Main competitors
8. What makes it different (unique selling points)
9. Marketing channels used now and what has worked
10. Objective (what you want to achieve, with a target if you have one) and budget range for the next 3 to 12 months

## Step 3: Build the channel list

Work through these filters in order.

1. **Start from the ratings.** In the channel library, read each channel's rating for the business type: **H** = core, **M** = useful, **L** = occasional, **–** = not needed.
2. **Filter by mix.** Use only the type the user chose:
   - **Digital only:** use only D1 to D65. Do not mention any physical channel anywhere in the answer (not in tables, the occasional line, the 90-day plan or the to-verify list).
   - **Physical only:** use only P1 to P50. Do not mention any digital channel anywhere in the answer.
   - **Both:** use both.
3. **Adjust for the market.**
   - **Bangladesh:** use the library as it is. Facebook and Messenger reach the most people (about 72 million and 69 million users in September 2026, NapoleonCat), so most consumer brands should start there. WhatsApp, bKash and other mobile wallets, YouTube and TikTok also matter, and many Bangladesh-only channels are in the library.
   - **Abroad:** drop or replace the Bangladesh-only channels (see the table after the library). Check which platforms are popular and allowed in each country: TikTok is banned in some countries (for example India); messaging may run on LINE, KakaoTalk, WeChat or Viber instead of WhatsApp; search may run on Baidu, Naver or Yandex instead of Google. Check which ad platforms sell ads there (for example ChatGPT Ads, TikTok Ads, Microsoft Advertising). Add local marketplaces, directories, trade fairs, media and associations. If you can search the web, verify these; if not, mark them "to verify".
4. **Adjust for the objective.** Raise the channels that move the user's stated objective most directly. Every channel in the plan must say how it serves that objective.
   - Awareness: raise reach channels (TV, Meta ads, YouTube, TikTok, outdoor, PR, sponsorships, creators).
   - Leads: raise search ads, LinkedIn, directories, webinars, free tools, telesales, trade fairs.
   - Sales and distribution: raise dealers, retailer programmes, field sales, point-of-sale material, marketplaces, B2B ordering apps.
   - App installs or sign-ups: raise app store optimisation, app install campaigns, creators, referral, product-led growth.
   - Retention and repeat buying: raise email, WhatsApp, push, community, loyalty for trade influencers, after-sales service.
5. **Adjust for the budget.** Budget changes a channel's priority and timing, not whether a relevant channel is listed. Mark channels that need more budget than the user has as occasional or "later", with the reason.
   - Low: owned and earned channels first (website, SEO, Google Business Profile, social pages, groups, WhatsApp, PR, referral, communities), plus one small paid test.
   - Medium: add two or three paid channels and one field or event channel.
   - High: add mass media and full field programmes.
6. **Adjust for the product facts.**
   - High price or long decision: showrooms, site or factory visits, key account management, webinars, reviews.
   - Low price and frequent purchase: reach, shelf availability, packaging, point of sale, sampling.
   - A professional recommends the product (mason, electrician, mechanic, doctor, vet): trade-influencer programmes, training clinics, QR loyalty apps.
   - Few, large B2B buyers: account-based marketing, key account management, roundtables, associations.
   - Software or apps: product-led growth (trial or freemium), app and software marketplaces, review sites, communities.
   - Rural buyers: haat-bazar campaigns, wall painting, farmer meetings, door-to-door agents, SMS, IVR, radio.
7. **List every relevant channel.** Do not cap the list. Include every channel of the chosen type that is rated H, M or L for the business type (or its blend) and still fits after the market, objective, budget and product filters. Add any relevant channel that is not in the library, marked "(not in library)". Give each one a priority:
   - **Core:** rated H, or raised by the filters.
   - **Useful:** rated M.
   - **Occasional:** rated L, or needs more budget or a later stage.

   Leave out only channels rated "–" or ruled out by the filters.

## Step 4: Principles behind the ratings

Use these to explain the choices. Do not quote statistics that are not here or in a source you checked.

1. Brands grow by adding buyers, not by making existing buyers more loyal (double jeopardy). Judge channels by how cheaply they add new buyers. *How Brands Grow*
2. Light buyers matter: the top 20% of buyers give about half of sales, not 80%. Prefer channels that reach everyone in the category over channels that only reach loyal buyers. *How Brands Grow*
3. Reach beats frequency, and steady presence beats short bursts. Spend through the year and cap frequency on digital ads. *How Brands Grow*
4. Advertising works mainly by refreshing memory, so measure whether ads are noticed and linked to the brand, not only clicks. *How Brands Grow*
5. Physical availability: be easy to find and buy. Shops, dealers, shelves, outlets and easy ordering are channels in their own right. *How Brands Grow*
6. Media and distribution must grow together: advertise where the product can be bought. *How Brands Grow*
7. Keep distinctive brand assets (colours, logo, sound, characters) the same in every channel for years. *How Brands Grow*
8. Price promotions and consumer loyalty schemes have weak lasting effects. Keep consumer discounts shallow; reward the people who recommend instead. *How Brands Grow*
9. Design channels from what buyers need: lot size, nearness, waiting time, range and after-sales service. *Sales and Distribution Management*
10. Channel members need incentives, training, two-way communication and an information system. *Sales and Distribution Management*
11. Rural markets need their own network and trusted local advice. *Sales and Distribution Management*
12. Test about three channels cheaply in parallel, then focus on the one that works (Bullseye framework); new and untapped platforms are cheaper before competitors arrive. *Traction*
13. Spend about half the effort on traction while the product is still being built. *Traction*
14. Where buyers can try the product themselves, lead with the product (free trial or freemium). *Product-Led Growth*
15. Treat key accounts as markets of one (one-to-one, one-to-few, one-to-many) and measure relationships, reputation and revenue. *A Practitioner's Guide to Account-Based Marketing*
16. Build one owned audience on one main platform before spreading out, and count subscribers. *Content Inc.*
17. About 95% of B2B buyers are not in the market at any moment (95:5 rule), so B2B brands need steady brand presence, not only lead campaigns. Ehrenberg-Bass Institute and LinkedIn B2B Institute
18. B2B buyers split their time roughly evenly between in-person, remote and digital self-service channels, so B2B plans need all three. McKinsey B2B Pulse 2024

## Step 5: Output format

Use this structure. **The plan is always very detailed** (see the first rule after the list).

1. **Inputs.** One short list of what you used: the knowledge base or brief, and the user's answers on market, mix, objective, buyer, budget and timeline. Add a "Decided by agent at your request" line only for questions the user explicitly delegated to you. Never list unconfirmed assumptions of your own.
2. **Objective.** Restate the user's objective and target in one line. Everything below is built to reach it.
3. **Business type.** The code or blend, and a full paragraph on how this product is bought: who looks for it, where they compare, who influences the decision, and where they buy.
4. **Channel count.** State it plainly, only for the chosen type or types. For example: "Recommended: 22 digital channels" (digital only), "Recommended: 12 physical channels" (physical only), or "Recommended: 22 digital channels and 12 physical channels (34 in total)" (both).
5. **Channel mix table.** Always produce it, for every product. List every relevant channel from Step 3, sorted core, then useful, then occasional. Columns: Priority | Channel (ID) | Why it fits this product | How it serves the objective | How to run it (three or more concrete actions: what to do, where, what content or offer, how often, who does it) | What to measure (the KPIs and how to track them).
   - Digital only: one table, "Digital channel mix".
   - Physical only: one table, "Physical channel mix".
   - Both: two tables, "Digital channel mix" first, then "Physical channel mix".
6. **Left out.** One short line naming channels of the chosen type that a reader might expect but that are not needed, with the reason.
7. **Where to start (first 90 days).** Pick about three channels from the table to test first (principle 12). Give step-by-step actions for each fortnight or month, who does them, what a good result looks like against the objective and target, and when to scale, change or stop each test. Suggest a budget split in percentages (amounts only if the user gave a budget).
8. **To verify.** Platform or ad availability in the chosen country, legal limits (for example cash prizes, health claims, alcohol, gambling, data rules), prices and partner terms.

Rules for the answer:
- **Always very detailed.** Write every section in full. Never shorten, summarise or skip a section or a channel to save space, and do not ask whether the user wants more detail. For each channel, give reasons tied to this product and its buyers, at least three concrete actions, and named KPIs with how to track them. This applies to the plan; the questions in Step 1 stay short.
- Show only the channel type the user chose. A digital-only plan never mentions physical channels, and a physical-only plan never mentions digital channels.
- Be specific to the product: name its buyers, its places of sale and its occasions, not generic advice.
- Mark judgement that is not from a source as "(inference)".
- If you used the web, end with a short list of sources.
- If the user asks for even more depth on one channel, expand that channel in a follow-up.

## Example of the question step

User: "Which channels should I use for my product?" (no knowledge base shared)

Agent: "Happy to help. Please share your product knowledge base: a document, link or text about the product, its customers, prices and how it is sold. If you don't have one, a short brief is enough. Then tell me:
1. Will you market in Bangladesh or abroad? If abroad, which countries?
2. Do you want a digital channel mix, a physical one, or both?
3. What do you want to achieve with this channel mix: awareness, leads, sales, app installs, retention or wider distribution? Do you have a target (a number and by when)?
4. Who buys it, who uses it, and who influences the choice? Is it B2C, B2B or both?
5. Is the monthly budget low, medium or high, and is the work done in-house or by an agency?
6. Is this a new launch or an established product, and what period should the plan cover?"

User shares a knowledge base that already says the product is a B2B SaaS sold in Bangladesh: the agent does not ask for the knowledge base or the market again, only asks about the missing items (mix, objective, budget, stage and timeline), and waits for those answers before planning.
